In 1965, Donald Davies noticed a waste in the telephone network. A computer
terminal connected over a phone line keeps a whole circuit reserved, and the
line stays silent most of the time. His answer, and Paul Baran's a few years
earlier, was to cut data into small blocks and let every block share every
link. The Internet is built on that decision, and it still explains why a
connection is fast at 3 a.m. and slow at 9 p.m. This post covers the
fundamentals that follow from it: packet switches, bit rate, forwarding and
routing, access networks, ISPs and the network core. Along the way it answers
one question with numbers: how can a network that reserves nothing serve 35
users on a link that circuits fill with 10, and what does it give up?

## The telephone network reserved a circuit for every call

A phone call uses **circuit switching**. Before the first word, the network sets
up a path and reserves a fixed share of every link on it for the whole call.
The reservation holds whether anyone speaks or not.

A link is divided among circuits in one of two ways. **Frequency-division
multiplexing** (FDM) gives each circuit its own band of frequencies.
**Time-division multiplexing** (TDM) divides time into frames and gives each
circuit one slot in every frame. A classic telephone trunk carries 24 circuits
of 64 kb/s each, 1.536 Mb/s of voice in total.

Reservation has a clear cost for data. Send a 640,000-bit file over one of those
circuits and it takes 640,000 / 64,000 = 10 seconds, plus the time to set up
the circuit. That holds even if the other 23 slots are idle. With the whole link
it would take 0.42 seconds.

Davies saw that computer traffic is **bursty**: short periods of activity
separated by long silences. He proposed to split messages into short blocks,
route each one independently and reassemble them at the destination. He called
them **packets**, after a linguist told him the word would translate well into
other languages. Paul Baran had reached the same idea at RAND, where he called
them "message blocks" in the reports he published in 1964. In later interviews,
Baran said that AT&T's engineers dismissed the idea.

The turning point came in October 1967. Roger Scantlebury presented Davies's
design at an ACM symposium in Gatlinburg, Tennessee, where the people planning
the ARPANET took notice, and the ARPANET was built as a packet-switched
network. On 29 October 1969, the first ARPANET message
went from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute over a 50 kb/s line. It was
the word "login", and the receiving computer crashed after "lo".

both with numbers.

## Packet switches store each packet, then send it at the link's bit rate

A packet is a header, which says where it goes, and a piece of data. The
devices that move packets are **packet switches**. There are two kinds:
**routers**, which forward by IP address, and **link-layer switches**, which
forward by MAC address inside one local network. The
[OSI post](/portfolio/blog/osi-layers-one-request/) follows one packet through
both.

Each link has a **bit rate** R, in bits per second: how fast its sender can put
bits on the wire. Pushing a packet of L bits onto the link takes L/R seconds,
the **transmission delay**. Then each bit travels the length d of the link at
the signal speed s, about 2 × 10⁸ m/s in fiber or copper. That is the
**propagation delay**, d/s. The two are easy to confuse. Bit rate is how fast
the cars enter the highway. Propagation is how long the highway is.

Most packet switches **store and forward**: they receive the whole packet,
check it, and only then start sending it on the next link. The next example
adds up both delays for a 1,500-byte packet on a five-link path, from a laptop
to a server 2,000 km away.

```python
import math

SPEED = 2e8  # meters per second, signals in fiber or copper (about 2/3 of c)
PACKET = 1_500  # bytes

# (link, bit rate in bits per second, length in meters)
PATH = [
    ("laptop -> home router", 1e9, 10),
    ("home -> ISP (access)", 100e6, 5_000),
    ("ISP metro", 10e9, 50_000),
    ("long haul", 100e9, 2_000_000),
    ("data center", 10e9, 100),
]


def transmit(size_bytes: int) -> list[float]:
    """L/R on each link: time to push every bit onto the wire."""
    return [size_bytes * 8 / rate for _, rate, _ in PATH]


one = transmit(PACKET)
prop = [length / SPEED for _, _, length in PATH]  # d/s on each link
print(f"one {PACKET:,}-byte packet       transmit L/R   propagate d/s")
for (name, _, _), tx, pr in zip(PATH, one, prop):
    print(f"  {name:<24} {tx * 1e6:9.2f} us {pr * 1e6:11.2f} us")
print(f"  {'total':<24} {sum(one) * 1e6:9.2f} us {sum(prop) * 1e6:11.2f} us")

FILE = 10_000_000  # bytes
packets = math.ceil(FILE / PACKET)
as_block = sum(transmit(FILE))  # each switch waits for the whole file
as_packets = sum(one) + (packets - 1) * max(one)  # links work in parallel
print()
for label, seconds in [("10 MB as one block", as_block),
                       (f"10 MB as {packets:,} packets", as_packets)]:
    print(f"{label:<26} {seconds * 1e3:6.1f} ms + propagation")
```

```text
one 1,500-byte packet       transmit L/R   propagate d/s
  laptop -> home router        12.00 us        0.05 us
  home -> ISP (access)        120.00 us       25.00 us
  ISP metro                     1.20 us      250.00 us
  long haul                     0.12 us    10000.00 us
  data center                   1.20 us        0.50 us
  total                       134.52 us    10275.55 us

10 MB as one block          896.8 ms + propagation
10 MB as 6,667 packets      800.1 ms + propagation
```

Three lessons are in that output. The slowest link, here the 100 Mb/s access
link, dominates the transmission time. Distance dominates for a single packet:
2,000 km cost 10 ms, and no bit rate can lower that. And small packets are
faster than one big block, because while one link sends packet 2, the next link
already sends packet 1. A 10 MB file then takes about as long as the bottleneck
link needs for it, 800 ms, instead of the sum of every link.

## Statistical multiplexing fits 35 users where circuits fit 10

Now the capacity question, with the classic example from Kurose and Ross's
textbook. A link carries 1 Mb/s. Each user sends at 100 kb/s while active, and
is active 10% of the time, independently of the others.

With circuits, each user needs a reserved 100 kb/s, so the link takes 10 users.
With packets, nothing is reserved. The link is only in trouble when more than
10 users are active at the same moment. The number of active users follows a
binomial distribution, so the probability is easy to compute:

```python
from math import comb

LINK = 1_000_000   # bits per second
PEAK = 100_000     # what one user sends while active
ACTIVE = 0.10      # each user is active 10% of the time, independently
SLOTS = LINK // PEAK  # users the link can carry at once: 10

print(f"circuit switching: {SLOTS} users, each with a reserved slot")
print("packet switching:")
print(" users  P(more than 10 active at once)")
for users in (10, 20, 35, 50, 70):
    # Binomial: probability that more than SLOTS of them are active.
    overload = sum(
        comb(users, k) * ACTIVE**k * (1 - ACTIVE) ** (users - k)
        for k in range(SLOTS + 1, users + 1)
    )
    print(f" {users:>5}  {overload:.6f}")
```

```text
circuit switching: 10 users, each with a reserved slot
packet switching:
 users  P(more than 10 active at once)
    10  0.000000
    20  0.000001
    35  0.000424
    50  0.009355
    70  0.087269
```

With 35 users, more than 10 are active only 0.04% of the time. Packet switching
serves 3.5 times as many users on the same link, because idle users leave their
share to the active ones. This effect is called **statistical multiplexing**,
and it answers the first half of the question.

## The price of sharing is a queue that explodes near full load

When more than 10 users are active, packets arrive faster than the link can
send them. The router holds the extra packets in a buffer, a queue, and sends
them in turn. Waiting in that queue is the **queueing delay**. When the buffer
is full, new packets are dropped. That is **packet loss**, and TCP reacts to it
by sending more slowly.

How long is the wait? It depends on the **load**, the fraction of time the link
is busy. The next example simulates 200,000 packets of one size, arriving at
random, at a router port. It uses Lindley's recursion: each packet waits for
whatever work was left when it arrived.

```python
import random

random.seed(3)
PACKETS = 200_000


def mean_wait(load: float) -> float:
    """Average wait in a router queue, in packet transmission times.

    Packets of one size arrive at random (a Poisson process); the link
    sends one packet per time unit, so `load` is the link's utilization.
    """
    wait = total = 0.0
    for _ in range(PACKETS):
        gap = random.expovariate(load)  # time until the next arrival
        wait = max(0.0, wait + 1.0 - gap)  # Lindley's recursion
        total += wait
    return total / PACKETS


print("load  simulated wait  formula  (in transmission times)")
for load in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99):
    formula = load / (2 * (1 - load))  # M/D/1 queue
    print(f"{load:4.2f}  {mean_wait(load):14.2f}  {formula:7.2f}")
```

```text
load  simulated wait  formula  (in transmission times)
0.50            0.50     0.50
0.80            1.96     2.00
0.90            4.43     4.50
0.95            9.64     9.50
0.99           56.56    49.50
```

The simulation matches the queueing formula for this case, ρ / (2(1 − ρ)), where
ρ is the load. At 99% load even 200,000 packets are not enough for the average
to settle, which is itself a sign of how unstable the queue is there.

The curve is the second half of the answer. Sharing is almost free while links
are lightly loaded, and it becomes very expensive as a link approaches full.
The units matter too. On the 100 Mb/s access link above, a transmission time is
120 µs, so 4.5 of them is about half a millisecond. Real queues are often
worse, because TCP keeps sending until a buffer fills. A full 1 MB buffer in
front of a 100 Mb/s link holds 80 ms of traffic.

## Forwarding moves each packet, and routing decides where packets go

Davies's design had a consequence for every router. A circuit knows its path
from the moment it is set up. A packet does not: it carries only its
destination, and every router on the way decides where it goes next. To make
that decision fast, a router does two different jobs on two different time
scales.

**Forwarding** is local and happens for every packet. The router reads the
destination address, looks it up in its forwarding table and moves the packet
to the right output link. It takes nanoseconds and is done in hardware. The
table holds address prefixes, and prefixes can overlap. The most specific
match, the **longest prefix**, wins:

```python
import ipaddress

# A forwarding table: prefix -> outgoing link. Prefixes may overlap.
TABLE = {
    "0.0.0.0/0": "link 0 (default, to the transit provider)",
    "10.0.0.0/8": "link 1",
    "10.20.0.0/16": "link 2",
    "10.20.30.0/24": "link 3",
}
ROUTES = [(ipaddress.ip_network(p), link) for p, link in TABLE.items()]


def forward(address: str) -> str:
    ip = ipaddress.ip_address(address)
    matches = [(net, link) for net, link in ROUTES if ip in net]
    net, link = max(matches, key=lambda m: m[0].prefixlen)  # longest wins
    return f"{address:<12} matches {len(matches)}, uses {net} -> {link}"


for destination in ("10.20.30.40", "10.20.99.1", "10.1.1.1", "192.0.2.7"):
    print(forward(destination))
```

```text
10.20.30.40  matches 4, uses 10.20.30.0/24 -> link 3
10.20.99.1   matches 3, uses 10.20.0.0/16 -> link 2
10.1.1.1     matches 2, uses 10.0.0.0/8 -> link 1
192.0.2.7    matches 1, uses 0.0.0.0/0 -> link 0 (default, to the transit provider)
```

**Routing** is global and slower. Routers exchange messages to learn which
destinations exist and which paths lead there, and they compute the forwarding
tables from that. Inside one network, protocols such as OSPF flood the map of
links to every router, and each router runs Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm
(explained, in Spanish, in the
[Dijkstra post](/portfolio/blog/grafos-6-caminos-mas-cortos/)). Between
networks, BGP carries reachability and policy: who pays whom decides which path
is used, not only which path is shortest. The scale is large. In May 2025, a
full BGP view held just over 1,000,000 IPv4 prefixes.

The split explains a common confusion. A router can forward millions of packets
per second with a table that changes only when routing changes it, every few
seconds or minutes.

## The edge reaches the core through access networks, and many are shared

The decision to share reaches all the way to your home. The **network edge** is
made of hosts, the laptops, phones and servers that run applications, and the **access networks** that connect them to their first
router. The **network core** is the mesh of routers and links that joins the
access networks to each other.

Access networks differ in medium, speed and in whether you share them:

| Connection      | Medium                       | Rates in the standard                    | Shared?                                        |
| --------------- | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| DSL (VDSL2)     | telephone copper pair        | about 100 Mb/s at 500 m, 50 Mb/s at 1 km | the line to the exchange is yours, then shared |
| Cable (DOCSIS)  | coaxial cable and fiber      | 3.1: up to 10 Gb/s down, 1 Gb/s up       | by the homes on one cable segment              |
| Fiber (GPON)    | fiber and a passive splitter | 2.488 Gb/s down, 1.244 Gb/s up           | by up to 32 or 64 homes on one splitter        |
| Fiber (XGS-PON) | fiber and a passive splitter | 10 Gb/s in each direction                | by up to 128 homes                             |
| Ethernet        | twisted pair or fiber        | 1 to 100 Gb/s per port                   | no, one port per device                        |
| Wi-Fi, 4G, 5G   | radio                        | depends on spectrum, distance and load   | by every device in the cell or network         |

The table repeats the lesson of the previous sections at the edge. The rate of
a home plan is a cap on your share of a link, not a reservation. On a fiber
splitter or a cable segment, upstream time is divided among homes by the
operator's equipment, a few milliseconds at a time.

## The Internet is a network of networks, joined by transit and peering

Sharing also happens between companies. No single company runs the core. It is
built from tens of thousands of independent networks, each called an **autonomous system**, and each run by an **ISP**,
an Internet service provider, or by a large company. They connect in two ways.

- **Transit.** A smaller network pays a larger one to carry its traffic to the
  rest of the Internet. Access ISPs buy transit from regional ISPs, and
  regional ISPs buy it from **tier-1** ISPs, networks with global reach that
  buy transit from no one.
- **Peering.** Two networks exchange traffic between their own customers,
  usually without payment. Tier-1 ISPs peer with each other. Many networks
  meet at **Internet exchange points** (IXPs), places where they connect to a
  shared switch and peer with many others at once.

<Figure caption="A network of networks. Access ISPs at the edge buy transit from regional ISPs, which buy it from tier-1 ISPs. Networks also peer, directly or at an Internet exchange point. A content provider runs its own network and connects close to its users.">
  <NetworkOfNetworks />
</Figure>

Large content providers run their own global networks. They connect directly
to access ISPs and at IXPs, so a video often reaches a home without crossing a
tier-1 network at all. BGP is the protocol that carries these business
relationships: each network announces the routes it is willing to carry, and
to whom.

## Sharing beats reserving until a link runs hot

The question had two halves. A network that reserves nothing serves more users
because bursty users are rarely active together. On a 1 Mb/s link, 35 users
overload it 0.04% of the time, where circuits stop at 10. It pays in queues. A
link at 50% load adds half a transmission time of waiting, a link at 90% adds
4.5, and a link near 100% adds without limit. Every other piece of the Internet
follows from that trade. Bit rate and distance set a floor on delay, forwarding
and routing move packets through shared links, and ISPs share links with each
other through transit and peering.

The takeaway is a test you can run today. When a connection feels slow, measure
its latency twice: once while it is idle, and once while a large download runs.
Many speed tests now report both. If the second number is much higher, the
bit rate of your plan is not the problem. The bottleneck link is busy, and its
queue is your delay. Routers with smart queue management, such as `fq_codel` or
CAKE, keep that queue short.
