Save $500 a month for 30 years at 8%, and you end up with about $745,180, even
though you only deposited $180,000. Borrow $300,000 for 30 years at 5%, and you
pay about $279,767 in interest on top of the loan. Both numbers come from the
same idea: **with a positive rate of return, a dollar today is worth more than
the same dollar received later.** That idea is the time value of money, and it
underlies nearly every financial decision, from personal savings goals to
corporate investments.

Money has a time value for three reasons. First, money can earn returns: a
dollar invested today at 5% a year grows to $1.05 a year from now. Second,
inflation erodes purchasing power, so future dollars buy less than today's.
Third, the future is uncertain: a promised payment next year may not arrive.

This article walks through the formulas behind those two opening numbers and
the decisions they support: choosing between a lump sum and installments,
evaluating an investment, planning for retirement, and comparing mortgages. All
rates and cash flows below are hypothetical, constant unless stated otherwise,
and exclude fees and taxes. See
[OpenStax Principles of Finance, time value of money](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance/pages/7-1-now-versus-later-concepts).

## Compound interest earns interest on interest

When you invest, you earn interest on the principal. Over time, the interest you
earn also earns interest. This process of earning returns on your returns is
called **compounding**, and it makes growth exponential.

The fundamental formula for compound interest is:

```text
FV = PV × (1 + r)^n
```

Where:

- `FV` is the future value of the investment
- `PV` is the present value (initial amount) of the investment
- `r` is the interest rate per period (expressed as a decimal)
- `n` is the number of periods

For example, if you invest $1,000 at a 6% annual interest rate for 10 years,
your future value is:

```text
FV = 1000 × (1.06)^10 = 1000 × 1.791 = $1,791
```

Your investment grew by almost 80%. The whole $791 gain is interest, and $191 of
it is interest earned on earlier interest.

We can rearrange this formula to work backwards. If you know the future value of
an investment and want its present value, use the present value formula:

```text
PV = FV / (1 + r)^n
```

This tells you how much money you need to invest today to reach a specific
financial goal in the future.

## The gap between simple and compound interest widens with time

To see what compounding adds, compare it with simple interest. With **simple
interest**, you earn returns only on the original principal. The formula is:

```text
FV = PV × (1 + r × n)
```

Using the previous example, $1,000 at 6% simple interest for 10 years grows to:

```text
FV = 1000 × (1 + 0.06 × 10) = 1000 × 1.6 = $1,600
```

With **compound interest**, you earn returns on the principal and on all
accumulated interest:

```text
FV = 1000 × (1.06)^10 = $1,791
```

The difference is $191 in favor of compound interest. Simple interest adds the
same $60 every year, while compound interest adds a little more each year, so
the gap widens with longer periods and higher rates. After 30 years, simple
interest reaches $2,800 and compound interest reaches $5,743.

Savings accounts and most investments compound, but loans apply interest in
different ways. Loan interest may accrue on the outstanding balance without
unpaid interest being capitalized. Check the actual contract rather than
assuming every product uses the same formula.

## Every cash flow is a lump sum, an annuity, or an irregular series

Financial situations involve three main types of cash flows: lump sums,
annuities, and irregular series of cash flows. Each requires its own valuation
approach.

### Lump sum

A lump sum is a single amount of money received or paid at one point in time.
It uses the two formulas above: `FV = PV × (1 + r)^n` to move it forward and
`PV = FV / (1 + r)^n` to bring it back.

#### College savings example

Suppose you want to save for your child's college education and estimate you'll
need $150,000 in 15 years. If you can invest in a fund earning 7% annually, how
much do you need to deposit today?

```text
PV = 150,000 / (1.07)^15 = 150,000 / 2.759032 = $54,367
```

You would need to deposit approximately $54,367 today to reach your goal of
$150,000 in 15 years, assuming a consistent 7% annual return.

### Annuity

An annuity is a series of equal payments made at regular intervals over time.
Common examples include mortgage payments, retirement withdrawals, and savings
contributions.

The **future value of an ordinary annuity** (payments at the end of each period)
is:

```text
FV = PMT × ((1 + r)^n - 1) / r
```

The **present value of an ordinary annuity** is:

```text
PV = PMT × (1 - (1 + r)^(-n)) / r
```

Where:

- `FV` is the future value of the annuity
- `PV` is the present value of the annuity
- `PMT` is the payment amount per period
- `r` is the interest rate per period
- `n` is the number of periods

#### Monthly savings plan example

You decide to save $500 per month for retirement in an investment account with a
hypothetical nominal annual rate of 8%, compounded monthly. How much will you
have accumulated after 30 years?

Use the exact monthly rate `r = 0.08 / 12` (approximately 0.6667%). If 8% were
an effective annual rate instead, the monthly rate would be `(1.08)^(1/12) - 1`.
These are different assumptions.

Number of periods: 30 years × 12 months = 360

```text
r = 0.08 / 12
FV = 500 × ((1 + r)^360 - 1) / r ≈ $745,179.72
```

By saving $500 per month for 30 years, you accumulate approximately $745,179.72.
Your total contributions are $500 × 360 = $180,000, so $565,179.72 comes from
investment returns: more than three times what you put in.

The formula also runs in reverse. If you want $500,000 at retirement and need to
know the monthly payment, solve for `PMT`:

```text
PMT = FV × r / ((1 + r)^n - 1)
PMT = 500,000 × r / ((1 + r)^360 - 1) ≈ $335.49
```

At the same 8% rate, about $335.49 a month for 30 years reaches $500,000.

### Series of cash flows and net present value

Many real-world investments involve irregular cash flows: different amounts at
different times. To evaluate these, we use **net present value (NPV)**, which
discounts every future cash flow back to the present and sums them.

The NPV formula is:

```text
NPV = Σ(t=0..n) CF_t / (1 + r)^t
```

Where:

- `CF_t` is the cash flow in period `t`
- `r` is the discount rate (required rate of return)
- `t` is the time period
- `n` is the total number of periods

A positive NPV means the modeled cash flows exceed the required return under the
chosen assumptions. For mutually exclusive, comparable projects without capital
constraints, higher NPV is preferred. Risk, unequal project lifetimes and
funding constraints can require additional analysis.

#### Investment project evaluation example

Your company is considering a project with the following cash flows over 5
years. The required rate of return is 10%.

- Year 0: -$100,000 (initial investment)
- Year 1: $30,000
- Year 2: $35,000
- Year 3: $40,000
- Year 4: $25,000
- Year 5: $20,000

Calculate the NPV:

```text
NPV = -100,000 / 1.10^0 + 30,000 / 1.10^1
    + 35,000 / 1.10^2 + 40,000 / 1.10^3
    + 25,000 / 1.10^4 + 20,000 / 1.10^5
```

```text
NPV ≈ -100,000 + 27,272.73 + 28,925.62 + 30,052.59
      + 17,075.34 + 12,418.43 = $15,744.70
```

The modeled NPV is $15,744.70, positive at the assumed 10% discount rate. The
project returns $150,000 in total, but in today's money those returns are worth
only $115,744.70. A positive NPV supports accepting the project if the forecasts, risk
adjustment and other constraints are appropriate; it is not a guarantee of the
actual outcome.

## Inflation shrinks what your returns can buy

When evaluating investments, distinguish between nominal returns and real
returns.

**Nominal returns** are the stated, unadjusted returns. If your savings account
earns 2% annually, that's the nominal rate.

**Real returns** account for inflation. If the nominal rate is 2% but inflation
is 3%, your real return is negative: your purchasing power declined.

The exact purchasing-power adjustment for the same period is:

```text
(1 + r_real) = (1 + r_nominal) / (1 + i)
```

Where `i` is the inflation rate. The commonly used approximation is
`real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation`.

For example, if your investment earns 6% (nominal) and inflation is 2%, your
real return is:

```text
r_real = (1.06) / (1.02) - 1 = 0.0392 = 3.92%
```

Your purchasing power increased by about 3.92%, not 6%. Over long periods, the
gap between nominal and real returns compounds too. To build real wealth, you
need returns that exceed inflation.

## The same formulas price mortgages and retirement

### Mortgage payments

When you take out a mortgage, the lender uses the present value of an annuity
formula to calculate your monthly payment. Take a $300,000 mortgage for 30 years
at a nominal annual rate of 5%, compounded monthly: `r = 0.05 / 12`, with 360
end-of-month payments. This excludes fees, insurance and taxes.

```text
PMT = 300,000 × r / (1 - (1 + r)^(-360)) ≈ $1,610.46
```

Using the unrounded payment, total principal and interest is approximately
$579,767.35, including $279,767.35 in interest: almost as much again as the
loan itself. Actual cent-rounded payments require a small final-payment
adjustment. Because interest accrues on the outstanding balance, paying extra
principal early shrinks the balance on which every later month's interest is
charged.

### Retirement planning

Retirement planning is fundamentally a time value of money problem. You need to
estimate:

1. How much you'll need annually in retirement
2. How many years you'll need the money
3. What return your investments will earn
4. How much you need to save now

If you need $50,000 annually in retirement for 30 years and expect 6% returns,
the present value of your retirement needs is:

```text
PV = 50,000 × (1 - (1.06)^(-30)) / 0.06 ≈ $688,241.56
```

Under this simplified model, the initial amount is $688,241.56: less than half
of the $1,500,000 you will withdraw, because the balance keeps earning while you
draw it down. It assumes fixed nominal withdrawals at year-end and a constant 6%
return, with no taxes, fees or inflation increases. Variable returns introduce
sequence-of-returns risk, so this is not a safe-withdrawal guarantee. Working
backward with the annuity formula, you can determine how much to save monthly to
reach this goal.

## Run the calculations in Python

These functions reproduce the examples above. Change the inputs to model your
own scenarios:

```python
def future_value(pv, rate, periods):
    """Calculate future value of a lump sum."""
    return pv * (1 + rate) ** periods

def present_value(fv, rate, periods):
    """Calculate present value of a future amount."""
    return fv / (1 + rate) ** periods

def annuity_future_value(pmt, rate, periods):
    """Calculate future value of an ordinary annuity."""
    if rate == 0:
        return pmt * periods
    return pmt * (((1 + rate) ** periods - 1) / rate)

def annuity_present_value(pmt, rate, periods):
    """Calculate present value of an ordinary annuity."""
    if rate == 0:
        return pmt * periods
    return pmt * ((1 - (1 + rate) ** (-periods)) / rate)

def net_present_value(cash_flows, discount_rate):
    """Calculate NPV of a series of cash flows."""
    npv = 0
    for t, cf in enumerate(cash_flows):
        npv += cf / (1 + discount_rate) ** t
    return npv

# Example: College savings, 150,000 needed in 15 years at 7%
deposit = present_value(150_000, 0.07, 15)
print(f"Deposit needed today: ${deposit:,.2f}")

# Example: End-of-month deposits; nominal 8% annually, compounded monthly
monthly_rate = 0.08 / 12
months = 30 * 12
monthly_payment = 500

fv = annuity_future_value(monthly_payment, monthly_rate, months)
print(f"Future value of retirement savings: ${fv:,.2f}")

# Example: Evaluate a project
project_cashflows = [-100000, 30000, 35000, 40000, 25000, 20000]
npv = net_present_value(project_cashflows, 0.10)
print(f"Net present value of project: ${npv:,.2f}")
```

## Move every cash flow to the same date before you compare

Money today has options. You can spend it, invest it, or lend it and earn
returns. Future money offers no such flexibility, because it is locked in the
future. Time value calculations translate future cash flows into their
equivalent present value so you can compare opportunities fairly.

The formulas are only as good as their inputs. Small changes in the interest
rate, inflation, or time period can significantly alter the results, so treat
them as guides and question your assumptions.

The practical rule is short: before you compare a lump sum with installments,
two investments, or two loans, bring every cash flow to the same date at the
same rate. Only then are you comparing like with like.
