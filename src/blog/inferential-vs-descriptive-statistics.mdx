Take seven measurements: 23, 45, 12, 67, 34, 89 and 21. You can say exactly what
they look like. You cannot yet say what the next measurement, or the whole
population they came from, will look like. The first job belongs to
**descriptive statistics**. The second belongs to **inferential statistics**,
and mixing them up is how a small sample turns into a confident, wrong
conclusion.

## Descriptive statistics summarizes the data you have

The goal of descriptive statistics is to **summarize a dataset with a small
number of measures**. Faced with a heap of raw numbers, it asks how we can find
the pattern and present it in a digestible way. It does this without assuming
anything beyond the dataset itself. The most common summaries are:

- **Central tendency:** the mean, median, and mode identify the center of the
  data.
- **Dispersion:** the variance, standard deviation, and interquartile range
  (IQR) describe how stretched or squeezed the data are.
- **Shape and relationship:** skewness, kurtosis, and the linear correlation
  between two variables.

For our seven measurements, the mean is 41.57 and the standard deviation is
27.71 (dividing by n - 1). Those two numbers describe the sample completely, and
they say nothing about anyone outside it.

## Inferential statistics estimates what you did not measure

Descriptive statistics describes the exact data on hand. Inferential statistics
**generalizes from a sample to a larger population**. Measuring a whole
population, such as every person in a country, is often impossible, slow, or
expensive. So researchers measure a sample and use a sampling design and a
statistical model to estimate the population parameters. A large sample alone
does not remove selection bias.

This works because **inferential statistics relies on the laws of probability**
to reach conclusions and to state how confident we are in them. It uses
**inductive reasoning**: the logic flows from the specific sample up to a
broader claim about the population.

The key techniques are:

- **Point estimation:** a single "best guess" for an unknown population
  parameter.
- **Interval estimation (confidence intervals):** an interval built with a
  specified long-run coverage rate under the model's assumptions. A 95%
  procedure covers the fixed population parameter in 95% of repeated samples.
  It does not assign a 95% probability to that parameter after you observe an
  interval.
- **Hypothesis testing:** an assessment of how compatible the data are with a
  null hypothesis and its assumptions. A _p-value_ is not the probability that
  the hypothesis is true, and statistical significance does not measure effect
  size.

## From seven numbers to a range for the population mean

Here is the step from description to inference. Assume the seven observations
are an independent random sample from a normal population. A Student's t
interval uses the sample standard deviation (`ddof=1`), six degrees of freedom,
and `mean ± 2.447 * sample_std / sqrt(7)`. With only seven observations, the
normality assumption matters. See
[NIST's confidence limits for the mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda352.htm).

The sample mean, 41.57, is a description. The range from 15.9 to 67.2 is an
inference. It is wide because seven observations carry little information and
the data vary a lot. A larger sample or less variable data would narrow it.

## Describe first, then infer, and state your assumptions

Descriptive statistics tells you what is in your sample. Inferential statistics
uses probability to say what that sample suggests about the population, and how
uncertain that suggestion is. Always describe the data before you infer from
it. Then check that your sampling and model assumptions hold, because the
interval is only as trustworthy as they are.
